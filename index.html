Sonification (Music)

Listen to the structure of the g₈₅ word. Slicing into chunks of 85 corresponds to exactly one morphism image per track. Because g(b), g(c), g(d) are cyclic permutations of g(a), each track shares the same set of pitches — only their order changes.

What to listen for: Each chunk of 85 letters is one “block” of the morphism. Because the images of a, b, c, d are cyclic permutations of each other, all chunks contain exactly the same pitch classes (C, E, G, A — a C major 6th chord). The Road to 4 Letters

How many letters does it take to build an infinite abelian square-free word? This question took over three decades to answer — click the events to explore.

Key milestones: Erdős posed the problem in 1961. Pleasants (1970) showed 5 letters suffice. Dekking (1979) proved 3 letters are impossible. The gap between 3 and 5 was finally closed by Keränen (1992) showing 4 letters are enough — settling the problem completely. Open questions remain about the exact number and structure of such words and morphisms.

Unfavorable Factors

Certain short words (“unfavorable factors”) cannot be extended far in either direction without creating an abelian square. Enter a word or pick an example, then see how far left and right the word can grow before it gets stuck. (Concept from Keränen’s 2010 Mathematica Journal article.)

Definition — Unfavorable Factor: An abelian square-free word w is an unfavorable factor if there exists a finite bound N such that every abelian square-free word containing w as a subword has length at most N. Such words act as “dead ends” in the search tree: no matter how you try to grow them, you will always encounter an abelian square within a bounded number of steps. Keränen used this concept in his 2010 paper to analyze the structure of the infinite g₈₅ word and show which local patterns are unavoidable.

Morphism Microscope

Two abelian square-free morphisms by Veikko Keränen. Both map each of the four letters to a longer word such that the image of b, c, d are cyclic permutations of the image of a. Iterating either morphism from the seed word “a” produces an infinite abelian square-free word.

Why are there multiple morphisms? Keränen’s g₈₅ (1992) was the first construction and remains the canonical one — length 85 per image is surprisingly long. Later work (g₁₀₉, 2009) produced morphisms with different image lengths and combinatorial properties, demonstrating that the language of abelian square-free words over 4 letters is rich enough to support multiple distinct morphic structures. The existence of many such morphisms is itself an open research area: how many fundamentally different abelian square-free morphisms are there?

Morphism Laboratory

Design your own uniform morphism over {a,b,c,d}. The program will evaluate its Parikh vectors, check for cyclic permutations, and iteratively search for abelian squares.

What makes a morphism abelian square-free? A trivial necessary condition is that each image must itself be abelian square-free. While many constructed morphisms use uniform lengths and cyclic Parikh vectors to restrict the search space, these are not mathematically necessary conditions. Finding a morphism whose infinite iteration avoids all abelian squares requires immense structural constraints — Keränen’s achievement was to discover these deep symmetries.

📖 AA2FR Research Platform - Quick Start Guide

1. Search Engine

Use the Start Extension button to start the background search. The algorithm automatically appends letters and backtracks when hitting dead ends. Try loading a 40-letter seed and run the search!

2. Predictive Lookahead

This is the program's most powerful "X-Ray Vision". First, Pause the search. You will see the letters displayed in the main view. Click any letter in the word! The program sends the sequence up to that point to the background engine, which looks 10 steps ahead. It will then tell you in the Predictive Lookahead panel whether the path is viable or a guaranteed dead end.

3. Manual Construction (New!)

You can now manually build the sequence based on the Predictive Lookahead results! Use the ✍️ Manual Construction buttons (a, b, c) under the lookahead panel to append letters. If a letter leads to an immediate abelian square or forbidden factor, it is instantly rejected and the reason is explained.

4. Search Pruning Heuristics

While the search runs and encounters dead ends, the Search Pruning Heuristics tracker on the right side logs them. If it notices that a specific suffix (e.g., bcca) causes an abnormally high number of dead ends, it automatically suggests it as a Structural Trap heuristic rule and provides a statistical confidence interval.

5. Research Notebook

Don't lose your findings! If the Heuristics tracker finds a trap, click Save to Notebook. If you manually discover a stuck path using Predictive Lookahead, click the 📓 Save Path button in the top bar. These will be permanently saved to your browser's memory in the bottom right corner.

Mathematical Context: Veikko Keränen proved in 1992 that an infinite fully Abelian square-free word exists over a 4-letter alphabet using an 85-uniform morphism. This AA2FR laboratory investigates an experimental research problem over a 3-letter (ternary) alphabet. Since 3 letters are mathematically insufficient to avoid all Abelian squares, we explore relaxed constraints:
  • Almost (A): We allow period-1 Abelian squares (aa, bb, cc), but strictly prohibit all other Abelian squares of half-length $\ge 2$.
  • Restrictions (R): We strictly prohibit 6 specific 4-letter factors: baac, caab, abbc, cbba, accb, bcca.
  • Validation Optimizations: We use Prefix Sum arrays to calculate Parikh vectors in $\mathcal{O}(1)$ time, and Integer Packing to compare them in $\mathcal{O}(1)$ time.
Long-term scientific goal: The objective of this platform is not merely to construct long AA2FR words, but to identify structural invariants, recurring motifs, and experimentally supported conjectures that may contribute to a future mathematical characterization of relaxed Abelian square avoidance over ternary alphabets.
